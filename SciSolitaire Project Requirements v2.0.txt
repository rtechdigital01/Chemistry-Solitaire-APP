1. Project Overview
SciSolitaire is a responsive web-based science learning platform built around a category-card matching game. Learners make connections between scientific terms, properties, processes, examples, symbols, formulae and diagrams by grouping related cards. The product is designed to feel like a puzzle rather than a conventional test, while still generating useful learning and progress data.
The public-facing brand is intentionally science-wide and internationally neutral. Chemistry, Biology and Physics sit under the SciSolitaire brand, while a learner's country, learning stage and curriculum choices determine which content is shown after sign-in.
1.1 Problem Statement 
Many learners struggle to engage with science when learning is mainly text-heavy (Gulzar & Ahmad, 2023), abstract (Oladejo et al., 2023), memorisation-led or repeatedly presented through conventional questions (Chengere et al., 2025; Arsyad et al., 2025). This can be particularly challenging for learners who benefit from visual, interactive, bite-sized or lower-pressure approaches, including some learners with SEND, disrupted education or barriers to sustained classroom engagement.
1.2 Product Goal
To create an accessible, engaging and curriculum-aware science platform that helps learners practise scientific concepts through matching, active recall and pattern recognition, while giving students and teachers useful information about progress and learning gaps.
1.3 Product Positioning
One international parent brand: SciSolitaire.
Three core science subjects: Chemistry, Biology and Physics.
Public pages use curriculum-neutral language such as secondary science, science learning and curriculum-aligned content rather than repeatedly displaying KS3/GCSE terminology.
Country- and curriculum-specific labels appear after profile setup, where they are useful to the learner and teacher.
The same core game engine should support multiple countries without requiring separate applications.
Accessibility is part of the core design rather than a later add-on.
1.4 MVP Content Decision
The application architecture should support all three sciences from the start, but content can be released in stages. Chemistry remains the first fully populated and tested subject for the initial playable MVP. Biology and Physics may appear as available or coming-soon subject areas until their content banks have been validated.
Within a subject, gameplay is topic/deck-based. The system should not depend on a single fixed UK curriculum structure or on a rigid five-level sequence. Difficulty and content selection should be determined by the learner's profile, topic, curriculum mapping and configurable game rules.

3.1 Role Selection at Authentication
Both the login and sign-up experience should clearly distinguish between Student and Teacher users. The selected role determines the fields shown, the dashboard destination and the permissions attached to the account.
Student sign-up: first name, last name, email, country, school/organization information, education stage/year group, password, required consent/terms fields.
Teacher sign-up: name, email, country, school/organisation information, password and required terms/privacy fields.
Google sign-in should still require first-time users to complete the missing role/profile fields before entering the application.
A user should not be able to switch from Student to Teacher simply by editing a profile field; role changes should be controlled.

4.1 Curriculum Mapping Rules
The same scientific concept may map to multiple countries, curricula and year groups without duplicating the core card content unnecessarily.
Country/stage mappings should control what the learner sees, not the public brand.
Content that is not appropriate for a learner's selected stage should not be prioritised in their default dashboard.
Teachers may need a broader preview mode so they can inspect content across stages when planning lessons.
The data model should allow new countries and curricula to be added without changing the core gameplay logic.
5. User Journeys
5.1 Student Journey
1. Student opens SciSolitaire from the website or a school-provided link.
2. Student chooses Log In or Sign Up and selects the Student role.
3. On first registration, the student completes profile details.
4. The student enters the Student Dashboard.
5. The student selects Chemistry, Biology or Physics.
6. The dashboard displays topics/decks appropriate to the selected subject and mapped learning stage.
7. The student selects a topic and starts or continues a game session.
8. During gameplay, the student receives supportive feedback, explanations, hints and rewards.
9. Progress, accuracy, attempts, topic performance and session history are saved.
10. At the next login, the dashboard highlights where the student stopped and what may need further practice.
5.2 Teacher Journey
1. Teacher clicks Dashboard or Log In from the public site.
2. If not authenticated, the teacher chooses the Teacher role and logs in or creates a teacher account.
3. The teacher completes required profile/school information on first use.
4. The teacher creates a class or uses the agreed linking method to add/associate students.
5. The Teacher Dashboard shows linked classes/students and a summary of activity.
6. The teacher can select a class, student, subject or topic to inspect performance.
7. The teacher can review accuracy, attempts, frequently missed concepts, completion patterns, hints used and recent activity.
8. The teacher uses these insights to decide what to revisit, explain or recommend in class or intervention sessions.
9. Teacher feedback on the platform/pilot can be captured for product improvement.
5.3 Dashboard Navigation Behaviour
If Dashboard is clicked by a user who is not logged in, route to role-based authentication.
If the authenticated user is a Student, Dashboard opens the Student Dashboard.
If the authenticated user is a Teacher, Dashboard opens the Teacher Dashboard.
The application should not expose teacher-only screens through public URLs without permission checks.

7. Dashboard Requirements
7.1 Student Dashboard
Display learner name and relevant profile context (for example: Science Learner • Year 9 • United Kingdom, or Science Learner • SS1 • Nigeria).
Provide a visible subject selector for Chemistry, Biology and Physics on desktop; a compact selector/dropdown may be used on smaller screens.
Show topic/deck cards underneath the selected subject.
Show only appropriate/available content for the learner's stage by default.
Allow Start Game or Continue Playing actions from each available topic/deck.
Display topic progress, accuracy, completion status and recent activity in a simple form.
Support a Resume/Continue area so the learner can quickly return to the previous topic or session.
Show earned rewards such as coins/badges where implemented, without making rewards the main learning objective.
Provide access to gameplay history and areas needing more practice.
Keep student-facing analytics understandable and encouraging rather than comparative or ranking-based.
7.2 Teacher Dashboard
Provide class and linked-student overview.
Allow filtering by class, student, subject, topic/deck and relevant time period.
Show individual learner performance including attempts, accuracy, completion and recent activity.
Show topic/category performance so a teacher can identify which scientific relationships are repeatedly misunderstood.
Highlight frequently missed concepts or repeated incorrect matches.
Show hints used and repeat attempts as indicators of where additional support may be helpful.
Allow teachers to open an individual student view without exposing data for students outside the teacher's authorised class/account.
Provide a simple feedback or notes area for pilot/product feedback where appropriate.
Support a simple downloadable or printable progress summary later if this can be implemented without delaying the core MVP.
Product principle: The distinguishing value of the Teacher Dashboard is not analytics alone. It should translate gameplay into practical concept-level insight that helps a teacher decide what to revisit or support next.

9. Gameplay Specification
9.1 Game Concept
SciSolitaire is a category-card matching game. A session presents a shuffled set of science cards and asks the learner to identify which cards belong together. Each correct group represents a scientific relationship or concept. The game should encourage retrieval, pattern recognition and conceptual connections rather than passive reading.
9.2 Core Gameplay
The learner selects a subject and topic/deck.
The game engine draws a configurable set of categories/themes from the selected topic bank.
Each category can contain a configurable number of matching cards; a typical bank may provide 4–7 suitable cards per category.
Cards are shuffled before the session begins.
The learner selects, drags or groups cards into the correct category/theme.
Correct groups lock or are clearly confirmed and award points/rewards.
Correct completion should provide a short explanation of the scientific connection where appropriate.
Incorrect selections should receive supportive feedback rather than a harsh failure screen.
After repeated errors, the game may offer a hint or reduce complexity according to rule-based support.
A session is completed when all required groups are correctly matched.
The result is saved to the learner's history and contributes to progress data.

9.4 Difficulty and Rule-Based Support
Difficulty should be configurable by learning stage, topic complexity, number of categories, number of cards, similarity of distractors and number of allowed hints/moves.
If a learner performs strongly, later sessions may use closer distractors or more complex relationships.
If a learner makes repeated incorrect matches, the system may offer a hint, explanation or simpler retry.
The initial MVP should use transparent rules rather than machine learning.
The wording 'Level 1', 'Level 2' etc. is optional and should not be required by the game engine; topic/deck progress can be used instead.
10. Accessibility and SEND Design Requirements
Simple layouts with limited visual clutter and predictable navigation.
Large cards, adequate spacing and clear clickable/touch targets.
Readable fonts, strong contrast and no reliance on colour alone to communicate meaning.
Clear keyboard navigation and visible focus indicators.
Screen-reader compatible labels for controls and meaningful alternative text for images.
Short, plain-language instructions with consistent wording.
Reduced-motion support and gentle animations.
Immediate, positive feedback after actions.
Optional hints that reduce frustration without giving away the entire answer immediately.
No harsh failure screens or punitive wording.
Learner-controlled pace and the ability to repeat topics.
Accessible error messages on login, forms and gameplay.
Mobile and tablet layouts should remain usable with the same core functionality.
Accessibility should be tested with learners and SEND practitioners, not assumed from design alone.
11. Data, Privacy, Safeguarding and Role Security
Because SciSolitaire may be used by children and young people, data collection and account access should be intentionally limited to what is needed for learning, product operation and approved evaluation.
Collect only necessary account/profile and gameplay data.
Use secure authentication and password handling appropriate to the chosen framework.
Apply role-based access controls at both interface and server/database level.
Teachers must only see students/classes they are authorised to access.
Student accounts must not expose other students' personal details or performance.
Teacher-student linking should use a controlled mechanism such as class code, invitation, school setup or approved association.
Sensitive or clinical information should not be collected as part of ordinary gameplay.
Privacy Policy, Terms of Use and student-information wording should be clear and accessible.
Age eligibility and consent requirements must be finalised before school pilots. If under-13 learners are permitted, an appropriate school/parent/guardian consent route should be implemented for the relevant jurisdiction.
Google authentication must not bypass role, consent or profile-completion requirements.
Research/evaluation datasets should be anonymised or pseudonymised where possible and handled separately from ordinary user-facing analytics.

17. MVP Success Criteria
A Student can create an account, log in and complete the required country/learning-stage profile.
A Teacher can create an account, log in and reach a Teacher Dashboard.
The same authentication area clearly distinguishes Student and Teacher roles.
A learner in the UK sees UK-appropriate stage labels, while a learner in Nigeria sees Nigerian school-stage labels.
A Student can select Chemistry, Biology or Physics and see the appropriate available/coming-soon topic/deck state.
At least one Chemistry topic is fully playable end to end.
A Student can complete a matching session and receive clear supportive feedback/explanation.
Progress and gameplay history persist across sessions.
A Student Dashboard shows current progress and a clear next/continue action.
A Teacher can securely link or access at least one authorised student and view that student's recent activity and topic performance.
Teacher views identify at least basic recurring errors or learning gaps from gameplay.
The application works reliably on desktop, laptop, tablet and mobile browser.
Core flows can be completed with keyboard navigation and without inaccessible colour-only cues.
The platform records the agreed basic usage data for pilot evaluation.
Student and teacher feedback can be collected during pilot use.
